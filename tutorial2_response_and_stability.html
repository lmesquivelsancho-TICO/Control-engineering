From poles and zeros to the shape of the step response

For each transfer function, find the poles and zeros, plot them on the s-plane, write the general form of the step response without inverting anything, and state the nature of the response.

\[\text{1. }\frac{2}{s+2}\qquad\text{2. }\frac{5}{(s+3)(s+6)}\qquad\text{3. }\frac{10(s+7)}{(s+10)(s+20)}\qquad\text{4. }\frac{20}{s^2+6s+144}\qquad\text{5. }\frac{s+2}{s^2+9}\qquad\text{6. }\frac{s+5}{(s+10)^2}\]

Nise, chapter 4, problem 8, p. 216. Sections 4.3 and 4.4.

The whole exercise in one table

A step input contributes its own pole at \(s=0\), so \(C(s)=\frac{1}{s}T(s)\) always has one more pole than \(T(s)\). Each pole of \(C(s)\) contributes one term to \(c(t)\); the zeros only change how big those terms are.

Walkthrough

One pole at \(s=-2\), no zeros. With the step pole added:

\[C(s)=\frac1s\cdot\frac{2}{s+2}=\frac{A}{s}+\frac{B}{s+2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad c(t)=A+Be^{-2t}\]

First-order response. No overshoot is possible; the time constant is \(\tau=1/2\) s and the final value is \(T(0)=1\).

Poles at \(-3\) and \(-6\), no zeros:

\[C(s)=\frac{A}{s}+\frac{B}{s+3}+\frac{C}{s+6}\quad\Rightarrow\quad c(t)=A+Be^{-3t}+Ce^{-6t}\]

Overdamped. Two real poles, so the response is the sum of two decaying exponentials and never overshoots. The slower pole \(-3\) dominates.

Poles at \(-10\) and \(-20\), and now a zero at \(-7\):

\[C(s)=\frac{A}{s}+\frac{B}{s+10}+\frac{C}{s+20}\quad\Rightarrow\quad c(t)=A+Be^{-10t}+Ce^{-20t}\]

Overdamped. The zero does not add a term: it only changes the values of \(A\), \(B\) and \(C\). Poles decide which exponentials appear, zeros decide how much of each.

The denominator has no real roots (\(36-576<0\)):

\[s=\frac{-6\pm\sqrt{36-576}}{2}=-3\pm j\sqrt{135}=-3\pm j3\sqrt{15}\approx-3\pm j11.62\]
\[c(t)=A+Be^{-3t}\cos\!\big(3\sqrt{15}\,t-\phi\big)\]

Underdamped. The real part \(-3\) sets how fast the oscillation dies, the imaginary part sets how fast it oscillates.

Poles at \(\pm j3\) (no real part at all), zero at \(-2\):

\[c(t)=A+B\cos(3t-\phi)\]

Undamped. Nothing multiplies the cosine by a decaying exponential, so the oscillation continues for ever: a marginally stable, purely oscillatory system.

A repeated pole at \(-10\), zero at \(-5\). A repeated pole needs two terms, one of them multiplied by \(t\):

\[C(s)=\frac{A}{s}+\frac{B}{s+10}+\frac{C}{(s+10)^2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad c(t)=A+Be^{-10t}+Cte^{-10t}\]

Critically damped. The fastest response you can get from two poles with no overshoot at all.

Damping ratio and natural frequency of the same six systems

Find \(\zeta\) and \(\omega_n\) for each second-order system of exercise 1 and check that the damping ratio agrees with the type of response predicted there.

Nise, chapter 4, problem 18, p. 216. Section 4.5.

Walkthrough

Every second-order system can be written in one standard form, and the two parameters are read straight off the denominator:

\[T(s)=\frac{\omega_n^2}{s^2+2\zeta\omega_ns+\omega_n^2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\omega_n=\sqrt{\text{constant term}},\qquad \zeta=\frac{\text{coefficient of }s}{2\omega_n}\]

Only the denominator matters: the numerator sets the gain, not the shape. And the damping ratio alone decides the type of response:

ζresponsepoles
\(\zeta=0\)undamped\(\pm j\omega_n\)
\(0<\zeta<1\)underdampedcomplex pair
\(\zeta=1\)critically dampedtwo equal real
\(\zeta>1\)overdampedtwo distinct real

Multiply the factors out first, then compare:

\[\frac{5}{(s+3)(s+6)}=\frac{5}{s^2+9s+18}\;\Rightarrow\;\omega_n=\sqrt{18}=4.243,\quad \zeta=\frac{9}{2\sqrt{18}}=1.061\]
\[\frac{10(s+7)}{(s+10)(s+20)}=\frac{10(s+7)}{s^2+30s+200}\;\Rightarrow\;\omega_n=\sqrt{200}=14.142,\quad \zeta=\frac{30}{2\sqrt{200}}=1.061\]

Both have \(\zeta>1\): overdamped, as predicted. The two damping ratios come out identical, which is a coincidence of the numbers — system 3 is simply system 2 scaled in frequency by \(10/3\).

\[\frac{20}{s^2+6s+144}\;\Rightarrow\;\omega_n=\sqrt{144}=12,\quad \zeta=\frac{6}{2\cdot12}=0.25\]

\(0<\zeta<1\): underdamped ✓, and \(\%OS=e^{-0.25\pi/\sqrt{1-0.0625}}\times100=44.4\%\).

\[\frac{s+2}{s^2+9}\;\Rightarrow\;\omega_n=3,\quad \zeta=\frac{0}{2\cdot3}=0\]

No \(s\) term means no damping at all: the poles sit on the imaginary axis and the response oscillates for ever ✓.

\[\frac{s+5}{(s+10)^2}=\frac{s+5}{s^2+20s+100}\;\Rightarrow\;\omega_n=10,\quad \zeta=\frac{20}{2\cdot10}=1\]

\(\zeta=1\) exactly: critically damped ✓, which is the same statement as “the two poles coincide”.

System 1, \(\frac{2}{s+2}\), is first order. It has no \(\omega_n\) and no \(\zeta\): those parameters are defined for a pair of poles. Its single parameter is the time constant \(\tau=0.5\) s.

The four transient specifications

For the second-order system below, find \(\zeta\), \(\omega_n\), \(T_s\), \(T_p\), \(T_r\) and \(\%OS\).

\[T(s)=\frac{0.04}{s^2+0.02s+0.04}\]

Nise, chapter 4, problem 20b, p. 217. Section 4.6.

Walkthrough

\[\omega_n=\sqrt{0.04}=0.2\ \text{rad/s},\qquad \zeta=\frac{0.02}{2(0.2)}=0.05\]

A very lightly damped, very slow system: the poles are at \(-0.01\pm j0.19997\), almost on the imaginary axis.

\[T_p=\frac{\pi}{\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}=\frac{\pi}{0.2\sqrt{1-0.0025}}=15.73\ \text{s}\]

Half a period of the damped oscillation. Since \(\zeta\) is tiny, \(\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\approx1\) and \(T_p\approx\pi/\omega_n\).

\[\%OS=e^{-\zeta\pi/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}\times100=e^{-0.157}\times100=85.45\%\]

The response very nearly doubles before coming back: with almost no damping, the first peak reaches 1.85 instead of 1.

\[T_s=\frac{4}{\zeta\omega_n}=\frac{4}{0.01}=400\ \text{s}\]

The envelope decays with \(\sigma=\zeta\omega_n=0.01\), so it needs 400 s to shrink to 2%. Note what this means physically: about 25 full oscillations before the response settles.

The exact 2% settling time of the simulated response is 380 s. The 4/σ formula tracks the envelope, which touches ±2% slightly later than the oscillation itself does.

Rise time has no clean closed form, so Nise fits a curve to the exact answer:

\[T_r=\frac{1.76\zeta^3-0.417\zeta^2+1.039\zeta+1}{\omega_n}=\frac{1.0511}{0.2}=5.26\ \text{s}\]

The simulated 10%-to-90% rise time is 5.31 s, so the fit is good to about 1%.

Read from the pole positions alone: \(\zeta=0.05\) fixes the overshoot (85.45%), \(\omega_d=0.2\) fixes the peak and rise times (15.73 s and 5.26 s), and \(\sigma=0.01\) fixes the settling time (400 s).

The one-line summary
The real part of the poles controls how long, the imaginary part controls how fast it oscillates, and their ratio (the angle) controls how much it overshoots.
Result
\[\omega_n=0.2\ \text{rad/s},\quad \zeta=0.05,\quad T_p=15.73\ \text{s},\quad \%OS=85.45\%,\quad T_s=400\ \text{s},\quad T_r=5.26\ \text{s}\]

Counting roots with a Routh table

How many roots of the polynomial lie in the right half-plane, in the left half-plane and on the \(j\omega\) axis?

\[P(s)=s^5+3s^4+5s^3+4s^2+s+3\]

Nise, chapter 6, problem 1, p. 326. Section 6.2.

Walkthrough

A fifth-order polynomial has no formula for its roots, but stability only needs a count, not the roots themselves. The Routh-Hurwitz criterion delivers that count from the coefficients alone.

First the cheap test: if any coefficient is missing or negative, there is definitely a root that is not in the left half-plane. Here all six coefficients \(1,3,5,4,1,3\) are present and positive, so the quick test is inconclusive and the table is needed.

Coefficients alternate between the first two rows, starting from the highest power:

\(s^5\)151
\(s^4\)343

Every entry uses the two rows above it: the first column of those rows, and the column one step to the right, divided by the first element of the row directly above.

\[b_1=\frac{3\cdot5-1\cdot4}{3}=\frac{11}{3}=3.667,\qquad b_2=\frac{3\cdot1-1\cdot3}{3}=0\]
\(s^5\)151
\(s^4\)343
\(s^3\)3.6670
\[c_1=\frac{3.667\cdot4-3\cdot0}{3.667}=4,\qquad c_2=\frac{3.667\cdot3-3\cdot0}{3.667}=3\]
\[d_1=\frac{4\cdot0-3.667\cdot3}{4}=-2.75,\qquad e_1=\frac{-2.75\cdot3-4\cdot0}{-2.75}=3\]
\(s^5\)151
\(s^4\)343
\(s^3\)3.6670
\(s^2\)43
\(s^1\)−2.750
\(s^0\)3

The first column runs \(1,\;3,\;3.667,\;4,\;-2.75,\;3\): it changes sign twice (\(4\to-2.75\) and \(-2.75\to3\)).

  • 2 roots in the right half-plane, one per sign change.
  • No zero row appeared, so no roots sit on the \(j\omega\) axis.
  • The remaining \(5-2=3\) roots are in the left half-plane.

The plot confirms it: the actual roots are \(0.256\pm j0.720\) (right half-plane), \(-0.941\pm j1.504\) and \(-1.631\).

Result
\[\text{2 roots in the right half-plane},\quad\text{3 in the left half-plane},\quad\text{none on the }j\omega\text{ axis}\]

A Routh table with a row of zeros

Determine how many closed-loop poles lie in the right half-plane, in the left half-plane and on the \(j\omega\) axis for

\[T(s)=\frac{s^3+2s^2+7s+21}{s^5-2s^4+3s^3-6s^2+2s-4}\]

Nise, chapter 6, problem 4, p. 326. Section 6.3.

Walkthrough

Closed-loop poles are the roots of the denominator, so the numerator plays no part:

\[P(s)=s^5-2s^4+3s^3-6s^2+2s-4\]

Negative coefficients already tell you the system is not stable. The table says how unstable.

\(s^5\)132
\(s^4\)−2−6−4

A row may be divided by any positive constant without changing the sign pattern, which keeps the arithmetic light. Dividing the \(s^4\) row by 2 gives \(-1,\,-3,\,-2\).

\[b_1=\frac{-1\cdot3-1\cdot(-3)}{-1}=0,\qquad b_2=\frac{-1\cdot2-1\cdot(-2)}{-1}=0\]

An entire row of zeros is not a dead end: it is a signal. It means the polynomial contains a factor whose roots are symmetric about the origin — pairs like \(\pm\sigma\), \(\pm j\omega\), or four points forming a rectangle.

Take the row above the zero row as coefficients, starting at the power in its label and skipping every other power:

\[P_{aux}(s)=-s^4-3s^2-2\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\frac{dP_{aux}}{ds}=-4s^3-6s\]

Use those coefficients in place of the zero row (dividing by 2 for convenience gives \(-2,\;-3\)) and continue as usual.

\(s^5\)132
\(s^4\)−1−3−2
\(s^3\)−2−30
\(s^2\)−3/2−20
\(s^1\)−1/30
\(s^0\)−2

One sign change (\(1\to-1\)): one root in the right half-plane.

The zero row said there is a symmetric factor, and the auxiliary polynomial is that factor. Solve it:

\[s^4+3s^2+2=(s^2+1)(s^2+2)=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad s=\pm j,\;\pm j\sqrt2\]

Four roots on the \(j\omega\) axis. With one root in the right half-plane, that accounts for all five, so none are in the left half-plane. Dividing out confirms it:

\[s^5-2s^4+3s^3-6s^2+2s-4=(s-2)(s^2+1)(s^2+2)\]

The system is unstable — one right-half-plane pole is enough — and it also carries two undamped oscillations at 1 and 1.414 rad/s.

Result
\[\text{1 pole in the right half-plane},\quad\text{4 on the }j\omega\text{ axis }(\pm j,\ \pm j\sqrt2),\quad\text{0 in the left half-plane}\]
Note on the posted solution

The last bullet concludes “\(5-1=4\) roots in the left half-plane”. That subtraction ignores the row of zeros it had just handled: the roots of the auxiliary polynomial \(s^4+3s^2+2\) are \(\pm j\) and \(\pm j\sqrt2\), all four on the \(j\omega\) axis, so the left half-plane is empty. The count of right-half-plane roots (one) and the conclusion that the system is unstable are right.

Can this closed loop be unstable?

For the unity-feedback system with

\[G(s)=\frac{K(s^2+1)}{(s+1)(s+2)}\]

determine whether the closed loop can be unstable.

Nise, chapter 6, problem 20, p. 328. Section 6.4.

Walkthrough

For unity feedback the closed-loop poles solve \(1+G(s)=0\), that is numerator plus denominator:

\[P(s)=(s+1)(s+2)+K(s^2+1)=s^2+3s+2+Ks^2+K=(1+K)s^2+3s+(2+K)\]

The zeros of \(G\) at \(\pm j\) pull the \(s^2\) and constant coefficients with \(K\), which is why the answer is not simply “large \(K\) is bad”.

\(s^2\)\(1+K\)\(2+K\)
\(s^1\)\(3\)0
\(s^0\)\(2+K\)

For a second-order polynomial the table just repeats the familiar rule: it is stable when all three coefficients have the same sign.

The first column is positive when \(1+K>0\) and \(2+K>0\), that is \(K>-1\).

  • \(-2<K<-1\): one sign change, one pole in the right half-plane.
  • \(K<-2\): two sign changes, two poles in the right half-plane.
  • \(K>-1\): stable.

So yes, it can be unstable — but only for \(K<-1\). For every positive gain the closed loop stays stable, no matter how large \(K\) gets. Try it in the lab, including negative values.

Result
\[\text{Unstable for }K<-1;\ \text{stable for all }K>-1,\ \text{in particular for every }K>0\]

The range of K that keeps the loop stable

For the unity-feedback system with

\[G(s)=\frac{K(s+6)}{s(s+1)(s+4)}\]

determine the range of \(K\) for stability.

Nise, chapter 6, problem 21, p. 328. Section 6.4.

Walkthrough

\[P(s)=s(s+1)(s+4)+K(s+6)=s^3+5s^2+4s+Ks+6K=s^3+5s^2+(K+4)s+6K\]
\(s^3\)1\(K+4\)
\(s^2\)5\(6K\)
\(s^1\)\(\dfrac{5(K+4)-6K}{5}=\dfrac{20-K}{5}\)0
\(s^0\)\(6K\)

Both remaining entries must be positive:

\[\frac{20-K}{5}>0\;\Rightarrow\;K<20,\qquad 6K>0\;\Rightarrow\;K>0\]
\[\boxed{\,0<K<20\,}\]

The lower limit comes from the constant term: with \(K\le0\) the polynomial has a root at or to the right of the origin, because the open-loop pole at \(s=0\) is never pulled into the left half-plane.

At \(K=20\) the \(s^1\) row vanishes, the signature of poles exactly on the \(j\omega\) axis. The auxiliary polynomial from the row above gives their frequency:

\[5s^2+6(20)=0\;\Rightarrow\;s^2=-24\;\Rightarrow\;s=\pm j4.90\]

So at \(K=20\) the system oscillates at 4.90 rad/s, and above it the oscillation grows. Set the slider to 20 in the lab and watch the step response stop settling.

Result
\[0<K<20,\qquad\text{marginally stable at }K=20\text{ with }s=\pm j4.90\]

The gain that makes the system oscillate

For the system shown, find the value of \(K\) that makes the system oscillate, and the frequency of oscillation.

Nise, chapter 6, problem 35, p. 329. Section 6.4.

Walkthrough

The inner loop has forward path \(\frac{1}{s(s+7)(s+3)}\) and feedback \(s\) (a rate feedback: it feeds back the derivative of the output). Using the feedback formula:

\[\frac{\frac{1}{s(s+7)(s+3)}}{1+\frac{s}{s(s+7)(s+3)}}=\frac{1}{s(s+7)(s+3)+s}\]

Multiplying by the gain block gives the forward path, and the outer loop is unity feedback:

\[G(s)=\frac{K}{s(s+7)(s+3)+s},\qquad T(s)=\frac{K}{s(s+7)(s+3)+s+K}\]
\[P(s)=s(s+7)(s+3)+s+K=s^3+10s^2+21s+s+K=s^3+10s^2+22s+K\]

The rate feedback contributed the extra \(+s\): it raised the coefficient of \(s\) from 21 to 22, which is exactly how derivative feedback adds damping.

\(s^3\)122
\(s^2\)10\(K\)
\(s^1\)\(\dfrac{220-K}{10}\)0
\(s^0\)\(K\)

Sustained oscillation means poles exactly on the \(j\omega\) axis, which happens when the \(s^1\) row is zero:

\[\frac{220-K}{10}=0\;\Rightarrow\;K=220\]

The row above then gives the auxiliary polynomial \(10s^2+220=0\), so

\[s^2+22=0\;\Rightarrow\;s=\pm j\sqrt{22}=\pm j4.69\]

The system oscillates at \(\sqrt{22}=4.69\) rad/s, a period of 1.34 s. Below \(K=220\) the loop is stable (and \(K>0\) is needed for the \(s^0\) row), above it the oscillation grows.

Result
\[K=220,\qquad \omega=\sqrt{22}=4.69\ \text{rad/s}\qquad(\text{stable for }0<K<220)\]
