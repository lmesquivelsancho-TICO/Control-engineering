Solve the differential equations with Laplace transforms

Solve each differential equation using Laplace transforms, with the initial conditions given. The forcing functions are zero before \(t=0^-\).

\[\text{(a)}\;\dot x+7x=5\cos 2t,\;x(0)=4\qquad\text{(b)}\;\ddot x+6\dot x+8x=5\sin 3t,\;x(0)=4,\;\dot x(0)=1\qquad\text{(c)}\;\ddot x+8\dot x+25x=10u(t),\;x(0)=2,\;\dot x(0)=3\]

Nise, chapter 2, problem 4, p. 98. Section 2.2.

The four moves that solve every one of them

MoveWhat it doesWhere it comes from
Transform\(\mathcal{L}\{\dot x\}=sX(s)-x(0)\) and \(\mathcal{L}\{\ddot x\}=s^2X(s)-sx(0)-\dot x(0)\)Table 2.2, items 7 and 8. The initial conditions enter here, and only here.
SolveThe equation becomes algebra: collect \(X(s)\) and divide.Linearity, Table 2.2 item 2 and 3.
SplitPartial fractions: one term per pole. A real pole gets \(\frac{K}{s+a}\); a complex pair keeps a first-order numerator \(\frac{K_1s+K_2}{s^2+bs+c}\).Section 2.2. Cover-up is the fastest way to get the constant over a simple real pole.
InvertRead each piece backwards from Table 2.1: \(\frac{1}{s+a}\to e^{-at}\), \(\frac{\omega}{s^2+\omega^2}\to\sin\omega t\), \(\frac{s}{s^2+\omega^2}\to\cos\omega t\), and with a shift \(\frac{s+a}{(s+a)^2+\omega^2}\to e^{-at}\cos\omega t\).Table 2.1 plus the frequency-shift theorem (Table 2.2, item 4).

Walkthrough

Left side, term by term: \(\mathcal{L}\{\dot x\}=sX(s)-x(0)=sX(s)-4\) and \(\mathcal{L}\{7x\}=7X(s)\). Right side, from Table 2.1: \(\mathcal{L}\{5\cos 2t\}=\frac{5s}{s^2+4}\).

\[sX(s)-4+7X(s)=\frac{5s}{s^2+4}\]

The equation is first order, so only \(x(0)\) is needed; the \(\dot x(0)=-4\) printed in the book is not used here.

\[X(s)\,(s+7)=4+\frac{5s}{s^2+4}=\frac{4(s^2+4)+5s}{s^2+4}\]
\[X(s)=\frac{4s^2+5s+16}{(s^2+4)(s+7)}\]

\(s^2+4\) has no real roots, so it stays in one piece and keeps a first-order numerator:

\[\frac{4s^2+5s+16}{(s^2+4)(s+7)}=\frac{K_1s+K_2}{s^2+4}+\frac{K_3}{s+7}\]

Cover-up for \(K_3\): multiply by \((s+7)\) and set \(s=-7\).

\[K_3=\frac{4(49)+5(-7)+16}{49+4}=\frac{177}{53}\]

Then pick two convenient values of \(s\). With \(s=0\):

\[\frac{16}{28}=\frac{K_2}{4}+\frac{177/53}{7}\;\Rightarrow\;K_2=\frac{20}{53}\]

and with \(s=1\):

\[\frac{25}{40}=\frac{K_1+20/53}{5}+\frac{177/53}{8}\;\Rightarrow\;K_1=\frac{35}{53}\]

Write the middle term so that Table 2.1 applies directly: \(\frac{20/53}{s^2+4}=\frac{10}{53}\cdot\frac{2}{s^2+4}\).

\[X(s)=\frac{35}{53}\frac{s}{s^2+4}+\frac{10}{53}\frac{2}{s^2+4}+\frac{177/53}{s+7}\]
\[x(t)=\frac{35}{53}\cos 2t+\frac{10}{53}\sin 2t+\frac{177}{53}e^{-7t}\]

Two checks that cost nothing:

  • \(x(0)=\frac{35}{53}+\frac{177}{53}=\frac{212}{53}=4\) ✓
  • The exponential dies out, so after a few time constants \(\tau=1/7\) only the 2 rad/s sinusoid is left. That is the steady-state response to the forcing; the \(e^{-7t}\) term is the natural response and it is the only piece that knows about \(x(0)\).
Result (a)
\[x(t)=\frac{35}{53}\cos 2t+\frac{10}{53}\sin 2t+\frac{177}{53}e^{-7t}\]

Now the second-derivative theorem is needed: \(\mathcal{L}\{\ddot x\}=s^2X-sx(0)-\dot x(0)=s^2X-4s-1\), and \(\mathcal{L}\{6\dot x\}=6sX-24\).

\[s^2X-4s-1+6sX-24+8X=\frac{15}{s^2+9}\]
\[X(s)\,(s^2+6s+8)=\frac{15}{s^2+9}+4s+25\]
\[X(s)=\frac{15+(4s+25)(s^2+9)}{(s^2+9)(s^2+6s+8)}=\frac{4s^3+25s^2+36s+240}{(s^2+9)(s+2)(s+4)}\]

The denominator factors because \(s^2+6s+8=(s+2)(s+4)\): two real poles from the system, one complex pair from the input.

\[X(s)=\frac{K_1}{s+2}+\frac{K_2}{s+4}+\frac{K_3s+K_4}{s^2+9}\]

Cover-up at the two real poles:

\[K_1=\frac{4s^3+25s^2+36s+240}{(s^2+9)(s+4)}\bigg|_{s=-2}=\frac{236}{26}=\frac{118}{13},\qquad K_2=\frac{4s^3+25s^2+36s+240}{(s^2+9)(s+2)}\bigg|_{s=-4}=\frac{240}{-50}=-\frac{24}{5}\]

Then match two coefficients. The \(s^3\) terms give \(K_1+K_2+K_3=4\), and the constant terms give \(36K_1+18K_2+8K_4=240\):

\[K_3=4-\frac{118}{13}+\frac{24}{5}=-\frac{18}{65},\qquad K_4=-\frac{3}{65}\]

Split the last piece into a cosine and a sine: \(\frac{-\frac{18}{65}s-\frac{3}{65}}{s^2+9}=-\frac{18}{65}\frac{s}{s^2+9}-\frac{1}{65}\frac{3}{s^2+9}\).

\[x(t)=\frac{118}{13}e^{-2t}-\frac{24}{5}e^{-4t}-\frac{18}{65}\cos 3t-\frac{1}{65}\sin 3t\]

\(x(0)=9.0769-4.8-0.2769=4\) ✓, and differentiating once and setting \(t=0\) gives \(\dot x(0)=1\) ✓.

The two exponentials are the natural response (poles at \(-2\) and \(-4\), from the system) and the sinusoid is the steady state (poles at \(\pm j3\), from the input). That split is exactly what the transfer-function view in the next tab makes systematic.

Result (b)
\[x(t)=\frac{118}{13}e^{-2t}-\frac{24}{5}e^{-4t}-\frac{18}{65}\cos 3t-\frac{1}{65}\sin 3t\]

Lab: move both initial conditions

x(t) in amber, its three terms thin

With \(x(0)=2\) and \(\dot x(0)=3\), and \(\mathcal{L}\{10u(t)\}=10/s\):

\[s^2X-2s-3+8(sX-2)+25X=\frac{10}{s}\]
\[X(s)\,(s^2+8s+25)=\frac{10}{s}+2s+19\;\Rightarrow\;X(s)=\frac{2s^2+19s+10}{s\,(s^2+8s+25)}\]

\(s^2+8s+25\) has discriminant \(64-100=-36<0\), so it cannot be split into real factors and keeps a first-order numerator:

\[X(s)=\frac{A}{s}+\frac{Bs+C}{s^2+8s+25}\]

Cover-up at \(s=0\): \(A=\frac{10}{25}=\frac{2}{5}\). Then match numerators:

\[2s^2+19s+10=A(s^2+8s+25)+(Bs+C)s\]
\[s^2:\;A+B=2\Rightarrow B=\tfrac85,\qquad s^1:\;8A+C=19\Rightarrow C=\tfrac{79}{5}\]

\(s^2+8s+25=(s+4)^2+3^2\), so shift the numerator to match the table:

\[\frac{\frac85 s+\frac{79}{5}}{(s+4)^2+3^2}=\frac{\frac85(s+4)+\frac{47}{5}}{(s+4)^2+3^2}=\frac85\cdot\frac{s+4}{(s+4)^2+3^2}+\frac{47}{15}\cdot\frac{3}{(s+4)^2+3^2}\]
\[x(t)=\frac25+e^{-4t}\left[\frac85\cos 3t+\frac{47}{15}\sin 3t\right]\]

\(x(0)=0.4+1.6=2\) ✓ and \(\dot x(0)=-4(1.6)+3\left(\frac{47}{15}\right)=-6.4+9.4=3\) ✓.

The final value theorem gives the same steady state without inverting anything: \(x(\infty)=\lim_{s\to0}sX(s)=\frac{10}{25}=\frac25\).

Result (c)
\[x(t)=\frac25+e^{-4t}\left[\frac85\cos 3t+\frac{47}{15}\sin 3t\right]\]

From a differential equation to a transfer function

A system is described by

\[\dddot y+3\ddot y+5\dot y+y=\dddot x+4\ddot x+6\dot x+8x\]

Find the transfer function \(Y(s)/X(s)\).

Nise, chapter 2, problem 7, p. 98. Section 2.3.

Walkthrough

A transfer function describes the system, not one particular experiment. It is defined as the ratio of the transformed output to the transformed input with all initial conditions zero, so every \(x(0)\), \(\dot x(0)\), \(y(0)\)… term from the differentiation theorem disappears.

That is also why the previous tab needed initial conditions and this one does not: there we solved one response, here we describe the system itself.

With zero initial conditions, each derivative becomes a power of \(s\): \(\mathcal{L}\{\dddot y\}=s^3Y\), \(\mathcal{L}\{\ddot y\}=s^2Y\), and so on.

\[s^3Y+3s^2Y+5sY+Y=s^3X+4s^2X+6sX+8X\]
\[Y(s)\left[s^3+3s^2+5s+1\right]=X(s)\left[s^3+4s^2+6s+8\right]\]
\[G(s)=\frac{Y(s)}{X(s)}=\frac{s^3+4s^2+6s+8}{s^3+3s^2+5s+1}\]
Shortcut
The coefficients transfer straight across: the left-hand side of the equation becomes the denominator, the right-hand side the numerator. No algebra needed once you trust it.

Poles are the roots of the denominator, zeros the roots of the numerator:

\[\text{poles: }-0.229,\;-1.385\pm j1.564\qquad\text{zeros: }-2.881,\;-0.559\pm j1.570\]

All poles have negative real parts, so the system is stable. The Routh first column (week 2) confirms it without factoring: \(1,\;3,\;\tfrac{14}{3},\;1\), all positive.

  • DC gain: \(G(0)=8/1=8\), so a unit step settles at 8.
  • Numerator and denominator have the same degree, so \(G(\infty)=1\): the output jumps to 1 the instant the step arrives. A direct path from input to output like this one shows up as a jump at \(t=0\).
  • The natural response is built from \(e^{-0.229t}\) and \(e^{-1.385t}\cos(1.564t+\phi)\); the slow pole at \(-0.229\) dominates and sets the settling time.
Result
\[\frac{Y(s)}{X(s)}=\frac{s^3+4s^2+6s+8}{s^3+3s^2+5s+1}\]

Transfer function of an electrical network

Find the transfer function \(G(s)=V_L(s)/V(s)\) for the network shown.

Nise, chapter 2, problem 17a, p. 99. Section 2.4.

Impedances: the same rules as resistors, once you are in the s-domain

ElementVoltage-current lawImpedance \(V(s)/I(s)\)
Resistor\(v=Ri\)\(R\)
Inductor\(v=L\frac{di}{dt}\)\(Ls\)
Capacitor\(v=\frac1C\int i\,dt\)\(\frac{1}{Cs}\)

With zero initial conditions, Kirchhoff's voltage law becomes a set of linear algebraic equations in \(I_1(s), I_2(s),\dots\): impedance times current, summed around each mesh, equals the applied voltage.

Walkthrough

Each 2 H inductor becomes an impedance \(2s\) and each 2 Ω resistor stays 2. Define one mesh current per loop, both clockwise: the shared 2 Ω resistor then carries \(I_1-I_2\).

Sum the impedance drops around the left loop:

\[L_1sI_1(s)+R_1\big(I_1(s)-I_2(s)\big)=V(s)\]
\[2sI_1+2(I_1-I_2)=V\;\Rightarrow\;(2s+2)I_1-2I_2=V\]

The right loop has no source, so the drops add to zero. The shared resistor is seen from this loop as \(R_1(I_2-I_1)\):

\[R_1\big(I_2-I_1\big)+R_2I_2+L_2sI_2=0\]
\[2(I_2-I_1)+2I_2+2sI_2=0\;\Rightarrow\;-2I_1+(2s+4)I_2=0\]

The second equation gives \(I_1\) directly in terms of \(I_2\):

\[I_1=\frac{2s+4}{2}I_2=(s+2)I_2\]

Substitute in the first equation:

\[(s+2)(2s+2)I_2-2I_2=V\;\Rightarrow\;\left[2s^2+6s+2\right]I_2=V\]

The output voltage is the drop across the second inductor, and only \(I_2\) flows through it:

\[V_L(s)=L_2s\,I_2(s)=2s\,I_2(s)\;\Rightarrow\;I_2=\frac{V_L}{2s}\]
\[\frac{V_L}{2s}\left[2s^2+6s+2\right]=V\;\Rightarrow\;G(s)=\frac{V_L(s)}{V(s)}=\frac{2s}{2s^2+6s+2}=\frac{s}{s^2+3s+1}\]

Three checks, all readable from the transfer function:

  • At DC (\(s=0\)) the gain is zero. Correct: a constant voltage makes both inductors behave as short circuits, so no voltage is left across \(L_2\).
  • At very high frequency the gain also falls, as \(1/s\). The network is a band-pass.
  • The poles \(-0.382\) and \(-2.618\) are real and negative, so the response is overdamped and stable — as it must be for a circuit built only from positive R and L.
Result
\[G(s)=\frac{V_L(s)}{V(s)}=\frac{s}{s^2+3s+1}\]

Lab: change the components

With general values the same two mesh equations give \(G(s)=\dfrac{R_1L_2\,s}{L_1L_2s^2+\big[L_1(R_1+R_2)+R_1L_2\big]s+R_1R_2}\). Set them back to 2, 2, 2, 2 to recover the answer above.

Transfer function of a translational mechanical network

Find the transfer function \(G(s)=X_2(s)/F(s)\) for the network shown. Both masses are 1 kg, the spring is 1 N/m, the damper is 1 N·s/m, and the surface is frictionless.

Nise, chapter 2, problem 24, p. 100. Section 2.5.

Walkthrough

Write Newton's law for the left mass. The spring and the damper both connect it to \(M_2\), so they react to the relative motion \(x_1-x_2\), and they pull back when \(M_1\) moves right:

\[M_1s^2X_1+f_vs\left[X_1-X_2\right]+k\left[X_1-X_2\right]=F(s)\]

With the given values, and collecting the \(X_1\) and \(X_2\) terms:

\[(s^2+s+1)X_1-(s+1)X_2=F(s)\]

Rule of thumb: the coefficient of \(X_1\) is the sum of everything attached to mass 1, and the coefficient of \(X_2\) is minus whatever is shared with mass 2.

No external force acts on the right mass, only the same spring and damper, now seen from the other side:

\[M_2s^2X_2+f_vs\left[X_2-X_1\right]+k\left[X_2-X_1\right]=0\]
\[-(s+1)X_1+(s^2+s+1)X_2=0\]

The second equation gives \(X_1\) in terms of \(X_2\):

\[X_1=\frac{(s^2+s+1)}{s+1}X_2\]

Substituting into the first equation:

\[\frac{(s^2+s+1)^2}{s+1}X_2-(s+1)X_2=F\;\Rightarrow\;X_2\,\frac{(s^2+s+1)^2-(s+1)^2}{s+1}=F\]

Same thing in one line. Writing the two equations as a matrix and solving for \(X_2\):

\[X_2=\frac{\begin{vmatrix}s^2+s+1 & F\\ -(s+1) & 0\end{vmatrix}}{\begin{vmatrix}s^2+s+1 & -(s+1)\\ -(s+1) & s^2+s+1\end{vmatrix}}=\frac{(s+1)F}{(s^2+s+1)^2-(s+1)^2}\]

The determinant is a difference of two squares, which factors neatly:

\[(s^2+s+1)^2-(s+1)^2=\big[(s^2+s+1)+(s+1)\big]\big[(s^2+s+1)-(s+1)\big]=(s^2+2s+2)\,s^2\]
\[G(s)=\frac{X_2(s)}{F(s)}=\frac{s+1}{s^2(s^2+2s+2)}\]

Nothing connects either mass to ground, so the pair is free to drift: a constant force accelerates the centre of mass forever. That shows up as \(s^2\) in the denominator, a double integrator, and the step response grows like \(t^2/\big(2(M_1+M_2)\big)\) instead of settling.

What does settle is the distance between the masses. The lab below plots both.

Result
\[G(s)=\frac{X_2(s)}{F(s)}=\frac{s+1}{s^2(s^2+2s+2)}\]

Transfer function of an electromechanical system

Find \(G(s)=Z(s)/V_{in}(s)\) for the voice-coil system shown, where the actuator force and the back-emf are related to the coil current and the mass velocity by \(F_{act}(t)=K_ti(t)\) and \(V_b(t)=K_b\dot z(t)\).

Tutorial exercise 5 (not from the book).

Walkthrough

The mass hangs on the spring and is pushed by the coil. There is no damper, so only three terms appear:

\[Ms^2Z(s)+kZ(s)=F_{act}(s)\]

One mesh, one current. Around the loop, the source drives the coil resistance, the coil inductance and the back-emf:

\[LsI(s)+RI(s)+V_b(s)=V_{in}(s)\]

These are what make it one system instead of two:

\[F_{act}(s)=K_t\,I(s)\qquad\text{(current produces force)}\]
\[V_b(s)=K_b\,s\,Z(s)\qquad\text{(motion produces voltage)}\]

The coil, moving through the magnet's field, generates a voltage proportional to its velocity \(\dot z\), which in the s-domain is \(sZ(s)\). Note the two constants have the same numerical value in SI units, which is why they are often both written as \(K\).

Solve the mechanical equation for \(I\) and substitute it, together with \(V_b\), into the loop equation:

\[I(s)=\frac{Ms^2+k}{K_t}Z(s)\]
\[(Ls+R)\frac{Ms^2+k}{K_t}Z(s)+K_bsZ(s)=V_{in}(s)\]

Multiply by \(K_t\) and collect \(Z(s)\):

\[\left[(Ls+R)(Ms^2+k)+K_bK_ts\right]Z(s)=K_tV_{in}(s)\]
\[G(s)=\frac{Z(s)}{V_{in}(s)}=\frac{K_t}{(Ls+R)(Ms^2+k)+K_bK_ts}=\frac{K_t}{LMs^3+RMs^2+(Lk+K_bK_t)s+Rk}\]

Set \(K_b=0\) for a moment: the denominator becomes \((Ls+R)(Ms^2+k)\), whose roots are \(-R/L\) and \(\pm j\sqrt{k/M}\). The mass would ring forever, because nothing in the mechanical drawing dissipates energy.

Putting \(K_b\) back adds \(K_bK_ts\) — a term proportional to velocity, which is exactly what a damper contributes. Motion pushes current back through \(R\), the resistor heats up, and the oscillation dies out. The lab has a switch for it.

DC gain: at \(s=0\), \(G(0)=K_t/(Rk)\). A steady voltage produces a steady current \(V_{in}/R\), a steady force \(K_tV_{in}/R\), and the spring balances it.

Result
\[G(s)=\frac{Z(s)}{V_{in}(s)}=\frac{K_t}{LMs^3+RMs^2+(Lk+K_bK_t)s+Rk}\]
