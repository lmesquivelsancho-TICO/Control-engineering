Analytical magnitude and phase

Find analytical expressions for the magnitude and phase response of

\[\text{(a)}\quad G(s)=\frac{1}{s(s+2)(s+4)}\qquad\qquad\text{(b)}\quad G(s)=\frac{s+5}{(s+2)(s+4)}\]

Nise, chapter 10, problem 1 (a, b), p. 610. Section 10.1.

Two routes to the same answer

Route 1 — rectangular form

Put \(s=j\omega\), multiply everything out, rationalise until \(G=\text{Re}+j\,\text{Im}\), then

\[M=\sqrt{\text{Re}^2+\text{Im}^2},\qquad \phi=\arctan\frac{\text{Im}}{\text{Re}}\]

Always works, but the algebra grows fast and the arctangent needs a quadrant check.

Route 2 — factored form

Keep the factors and use the fact that magnitudes multiply and angles add:

\[M=\frac{\prod|\text{zero factors}|}{\prod|\text{pole factors}|},\qquad \phi=\sum\angle\text{zeros}-\sum\angle\text{poles}\]

Each factor \(j\omega+a\) contributes \(\sqrt{a^2+\omega^2}\) and \(\arctan(\omega/a)\). No rationalising, no quadrant trouble.

Walkthrough

\[G(j\omega)=\frac{1}{j\omega(j\omega+2)(j\omega+4)}=\frac{1}{j\omega(-\omega^2+6j\omega+8)}=\frac{1}{-6\omega^2+j(8\omega-\omega^3)}\]

Both the real part \(-6\omega^2\) and the imaginary part \(8\omega-\omega^3\) came out of the same product; keep them apart from here on.

The magnitude of a quotient is the quotient of the magnitudes:

\[M=|G(j\omega)|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{36\omega^4+(8\omega-\omega^3)^2}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\omega^6+20\omega^4+64\omega^2}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\omega^2(\omega^4+20\omega^2+64)}}\]

and since \(\omega^4+20\omega^2+64=(\omega^2+4)(\omega^2+16)\), this is the same as

\[M=\frac{1}{\omega\sqrt{\omega^2+4}\,\sqrt{\omega^2+16}}\]

The phase of \(1/D\) is minus the phase of \(D\). Since \(\text{Re}(D)=-6\omega^2\) is always negative, \(D\) never lies in the right half of the complex plane, and a plain \(\arctan\) of the ratio would land in the wrong quadrant — hence the \(-180^\circ\):

\[\phi=\arctan\!\left[\frac{8\omega-\omega^3}{6\omega^2}\right]-180^\circ\]

Check the ends: as \(\omega\to0\) the arctangent tends to \(+90^\circ\), so \(\phi\to-90^\circ\); as \(\omega\to\infty\) it tends to \(-90^\circ\), so \(\phi\to-270^\circ\). Three poles, \(-270^\circ\) of total phase lag: exactly what you expect.

Reading the factored form directly:

\[M=\frac{1}{|j\omega|\cdot|j\omega+2|\cdot|j\omega+4|}=\frac{1}{\omega\sqrt{4+\omega^2}\sqrt{16+\omega^2}}\]
\[\phi=-\angle j\omega-\angle(j\omega+2)-\angle(j\omega+4)=-90^\circ-\arctan\frac{\omega}{2}-\arctan\frac{\omega}{4}\]

Identical numbers, a fraction of the work, and no quadrant discussion: each term's angle is already in the right range.

At \(\omega=2\):

\[M=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{8}\sqrt{20}}=0.03953\;(-28.06\text{ dB}),\qquad \phi=-90^\circ-45^\circ-26.57^\circ=-161.57^\circ\]

The lab below does this graphically for any frequency you like.

\[G(j\omega)=\frac{j\omega+5}{(j\omega+2)(j\omega+4)}=\frac{j\omega+5}{(8-\omega^2)+6j\omega}\]

Multiplying above and below by the conjugate of the denominator:

\[G(j\omega)=\frac{(\omega^2+40)-j(\omega^3+22\omega)}{\omega^4+20\omega^2+64}\]
\[M=\frac{\sqrt{(\omega^2+40)^2+(\omega^3+22\omega)^2}}{\omega^4+20\omega^2+64}\]

which, once you notice \((\omega^2+40)^2+\omega^2(\omega^2+22)^2=(\omega^2+25)(\omega^4+20\omega^2+64)\), collapses to

\[M=\frac{\sqrt{\omega^2+25}}{\sqrt{\omega^2+4}\,\sqrt{\omega^2+16}}\]

The denominator of the rationalised form is real and positive, so the quadrant is settled by the numerator: positive real part, negative imaginary part, hence a phase between \(0^\circ\) and \(-90^\circ\):

\[\phi=-\arctan\!\left[\frac{\omega^3+22\omega}{\omega^2+40}\right]\]
\[M=\frac{|j\omega+5|}{|j\omega+2|\,|j\omega+4|}=\frac{\sqrt{25+\omega^2}}{\sqrt{4+\omega^2}\sqrt{16+\omega^2}}\]
\[\phi=\arctan\frac{\omega}{5}-\arctan\frac{\omega}{2}-\arctan\frac{\omega}{4}\]

The zero contributes a positive angle, the poles negative ones. At low frequency they nearly cancel, which is why this system's phase never goes far below \(-90^\circ\).

At \(\omega=3\): \(M=\sqrt{34}/(\sqrt{13}\sqrt{25})=0.3234\;(-9.81\text{ dB})\) and

\[\phi=30.96^\circ-56.31^\circ-36.87^\circ=-62.22^\circ\]

The rationalised expression gives \(-\arctan(93/49)=-62.22^\circ\) ✓ — same number, more algebra.

Result
\[\text{(a)}\quad M=\frac{1}{\omega\sqrt{\omega^2+4}\sqrt{\omega^2+16}},\qquad \phi=-90^\circ-\arctan\frac{\omega}{2}-\arctan\frac{\omega}{4}\] \[\text{(b)}\quad M=\frac{\sqrt{\omega^2+25}}{\sqrt{\omega^2+4}\sqrt{\omega^2+16}},\qquad \phi=\arctan\frac{\omega}{5}-\arctan\frac{\omega}{2}-\arctan\frac{\omega}{4}\]

Notes on the posted solution

Asymptotic Bode sketches

For each function of exercise 1, sketch the asymptotic Bode magnitude and phase plots.

Nise, chapter 10, problem 4, p. 610. Section 10.2.

The four building blocks

First put every factor in the normalised form \(\left(\frac{j\omega}{a}+1\right)\), so that each term is 1 at low frequency and the leading constant carries the whole DC gain. Then add the pieces — in dB for the magnitude, in degrees for the phase.

Walkthrough

\[G(j\omega)=\frac{1}{j\omega(j\omega+2)(j\omega+4)}=\frac{1}{8\,j\omega\left(\frac{j\omega}{2}+1\right)\left(\frac{j\omega}{4}+1\right)}\]

Four terms: the constant \(\frac18\), a pole at the origin, a pole breaking at 2 and a pole breaking at 4.

\[20\log_{10}(0.125)=-18.06\text{ dB}\]

A positive constant contributes nothing to the phase.

\(1/j\omega\) has magnitude \(1/\omega\), which in dB is a straight line of slope −20 dB/decade crossing 0 dB at \(\omega=1\). Its phase is a constant −90°, at every frequency.

Together with the constant, the low-frequency asymptote of the whole plot is therefore \(-18.06\) dB at \(\omega=1\) sloping down at 20 dB/decade — the amber line.

Break frequency 2. Below it this term adds nothing; above it the slope steepens by another −20 dB/decade, so the total becomes −40 dB/decade.

Its phase asymptote starts at \(\omega=0.2\), falls at −45°/decade and flattens out at −90° from \(\omega=20\) onwards.

Same shape, break frequency 4: the magnitude slope goes to −60 dB/decade above it, and the phase contributes another −45°/decade between \(\omega=0.4\) and \(\omega=40\).

And the phase, corner by corner: \(-90^\circ\) up to \(\omega=0.2\); \(-45^\circ\)/decade from 0.2 to 0.4; \(-90^\circ\)/decade from 0.4 to 20 (both poles contributing); \(-45^\circ\)/decade from 20 to 40; then flat at \(-270^\circ\).

The white curve is the exact response. The asymptotes miss it by 3 dB at each break frequency and by a few degrees at the phase corners — close enough for design work, which is the whole point of the sketch.

\[G(j\omega)=\frac{j\omega+5}{(j\omega+2)(j\omega+4)}=\frac{5\left(\frac{j\omega}{5}+1\right)}{2\cdot4\left(\frac{j\omega}{2}+1\right)\left(\frac{j\omega}{4}+1\right)}=\frac58\cdot\frac{\frac{j\omega}{5}+1}{\left(\frac{j\omega}{2}+1\right)\left(\frac{j\omega}{4}+1\right)}\]

Four terms again: the constant \(\frac58\;(-4.08\) dB), a zero at 5, poles at 2 and 4. There is no pole at the origin this time, so the plot starts flat.

A zero is a pole with the signs flipped: above its break frequency of 5 the magnitude asymptote turns up by 20 dB/decade, and the phase climbs \(+45^\circ\)/decade between \(\omega=0.5\) and \(\omega=50\).

So the plot leaves \(-4.08\) dB flat, tips down at 2, steepens at 4, and is pulled back to a single −20 dB/decade slope by the zero at 5 — which is the correct final slope, since the transfer function has two poles and one zero.

Corners at \(0.2,\;0.4,\;0.5,\;20,\;40,\;50\). The two poles pull the phase down to \(-180^\circ\), the zero pushes \(+90^\circ\) back up, so the curve ends at \(-90^\circ\) — as it must, for one more pole than zeros.

In between, the phase dips to about \(-64^\circ\) around \(\omega=4\) and comes back up. That non-monotonic shape is the visible signature of a zero sitting close to the poles.

Stability from the Nyquist plot

Explain how the stability of the closed-loop system can be determined from the Nyquist plot.

Tutorial exercise 3.

Walkthrough

Take the contour that wraps the whole right half of the s-plane — up the imaginary axis from \(-j\infty\) to \(+j\infty\), then round by an infinite semicircle — and map every point of it through \(G(s)H(s)\). The picture that comes out is the Nyquist plot.

In practice you only draw \(G(j\omega)\) for \(\omega:0\to\infty\); the negative frequencies are its mirror image in the real axis, and for a strictly proper \(G\) the infinite semicircle maps to the origin. If \(G\) has poles on the imaginary axis (an integrator, say) the contour makes a small detour to the right around them, and that detour maps to a large arc.

Closed-loop poles are zeros of \(1+G(s)H(s)\), so counting them inside the contour is a job for the argument principle. Writing

\[Z=N+P\]
  • \(Z\) — closed-loop poles in the right half-plane. Stable means \(Z=0\).
  • \(N\) — the number of clockwise encirclements of the point \(-1\) by the Nyquist plot (counter-clockwise ones count as negative).
  • \(P\) — the number of open-loop poles already in the right half-plane, read off \(G(s)H(s)\) before the loop is closed.

Why \(-1\): the plot of \(GH\) encircling \(-1\) is the same as the plot of \(1+GH\) encircling the origin, and the origin is where the closed-loop poles live.

If the open-loop system is itself stable, \(P=0\), and the criterion collapses to \(Z=N\): the closed loop is stable exactly when the plot does not encircle \(-1\). That is the common case, and the one the posted answer describes.

It is not the general case. With \(P=1\) the plot must encircle \(-1\) once counter-clockwise (\(N=-1\)) to give \(Z=0\). Avoiding \(-1\) there would leave \(Z=1\) — unstable. The second system in the lab is exactly that situation.

Take \(G(s)=\dfrac{K}{s(s+2)(s+4)}\), unity feedback. It has no right-half-plane poles, so \(P=0\).

The plot crosses the negative real axis where the imaginary part vanishes, i.e. where \(8\omega-\omega^3=0\), so at \(\omega=\sqrt8=2.83\) rad/s. There

\[G(j2.83)=\frac{K}{-6(8)}=-\frac{K}{48}\]

The crossing reaches \(-1\) when \(K=48\). Below that the plot does not enclose \(-1\) and the loop is stable; above it there are two clockwise encirclements, so \(Z=2\).

Compare with Routh on \(s^3+6s^2+8s+K\): the \(s^1\) row is \(\frac{48-K}{6}\), stable for \(0<K<48\). Two completely different methods, the same number.

The same plot measures how close to instability you are:

  • Gain margin — the factor by which \(K\) can grow before the crossing reaches \(-1\). Here, at \(K=20\), the crossing sits at \(-20/48=-0.417\), so the gain margin is \(1/0.417=2.4\) (7.6 dB).
  • Phase margin — the extra phase lag that would push the point where \(|GH|=1\) onto \(-1\), measured as the angle from \(-180^\circ\) at that frequency.

Both are read straight off the Bode plots of the previous tab as well, which is why the frequency-response view is so useful in design.

Result

The closed loop is stable when \(Z=N+P=0\): the Nyquist plot of \(GH\) must encircle \(-1\) exactly \(P\) times counter-clockwise, where \(P\) is the number of open-loop poles in the right half-plane. For an open-loop stable system (\(P=0\)) this means simply: do not encircle \(-1\).
